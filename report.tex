\section{Architecture Comparison: Bi-Encoder vs. Cross-Encoder}

In this assignment, I implemented and evaluated two retrieval architectures for Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG): a single-stage dense Bi-Encoder baseline and a two-stage pipeline combining the Bi-Encoder with a Cross-Encoder re-ranker.

\textbf{Bi-Encoder (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}):} A Bi-Encoder processes the query $q$ and document chunks $c$ through independent transformer passes, generating separate 384-dimensional dense vectors. Relevance is calculated via geometric cosine similarity between the two embeddings. Because chunk vectors are completely independent of any future query, the entire corpus can be precomputed offline and indexed in an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) graph (like HNSW in FAISS), enabling sub-linear searches in a few milliseconds. However, compressing an entire 200-word policy paragraph into a single vector creates an informational bottleneck. Query tokens and chunk tokens never interact directly in attention, which means fine-grained constraints, exceptions, and negations often get lost.

\textbf{Cross-Encoder (\texttt{ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2}):} A Cross-Encoder concatenates the query and candidate chunk into a single input sequence $(\text{[CLS]} \circ q \circ \text{[SEP]} \circ c \circ \text{[SEP]})$ and runs it through full transformer self-attention. Every query token can attend directly to every chunk token across all layers. This all-to-all cross-attention allows the model to spot exact conditional qualifiers (such as specific flight hour thresholds) and negative constraints (such as non-reimbursable meal items). The core tradeoff is computational: query and chunk cannot be decoupled, so offline precomputation is impossible and every candidate pair requires a full forward pass at runtime.

\section{Qualitative Retrieval \& Rank Shift Analysis}

\subsection{Evaluation Document \& Experimental Setup}
I constructed a synthetic 4-page corporate compliance manual titled \textit{``Acme Global Travel, Expense and Procurement Policy (2026)''} containing 30 structured sections. It contains realistic enterprise edge cases: a general coach airfare mandate with distant exception rules, meal entertainment caps alongside distractor hotel incidentals, and explicit late-submission forfeiture penalties.

To evaluate both pipelines, I designed a 6-query test suite. Tests 1 and 2 explore distractor and context ordering effects where the target was retrieved at Rank \#3 in the baseline. Tests 3 and 4 evaluate genuine retrieval omission failures where the critical section was buried outside the Top 3 (ranks 5 and 8) in the baseline and promoted into the Top 3 by the Cross-Encoder. Tests 5 and 6 serve as stable controls. The local generator is \texttt{Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct}, an open-weights 0.5B parameter LLM run locally on CPU.

\subsection{Detailed Case Study 1: Exceptions \& Distractor Chunk (TEST\_01)}
\textbf{Query:} \textit{``Can staff fly in premium cabins for international client negotiations?''}\\
\textbf{Target Rule:} Section 2.4 allows Business Class on continuous non-stop international flights exceeding 8 hours (or exceeding 6 hours if presenting or negotiating within 12 hours of arrival), with written VP and CFO approval.

In the baseline Bi-Encoder, Section 2.1 (\textit{Standard Cabin Class Mandate}) ranked at \#1 (cosine 0.5428), Section 1.3 was \#2 (0.4127), and the target exception Section 2.4 placed at Rank \#3 (0.4039). Because Section 2.4 was in the Top 3 context, the baseline LLM did see it. However, the baseline answer stated:
\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Answer 1 (Baseline):} \textit{``Yes, staff can fly in premium cabins for international client negotiations. However, they need to obtain prior written concurrence from both the Division Vice President and the Chief Financial Officer at least 10 business days before ticketing.''}
\end{quote}
Answer 1 is not factually incorrect, but it missed the flight duration thresholds ($>$ 8 hours, or $>$ 6 hours for negotiations). Because our generator is a compact 0.5B model, having the general coach mandate at Rank \#1 overshadowed the conditional numbers in the third chunk. A stronger frontier model might have synthesized the full answer from this context, but for a 0.5B model, chunk ordering matters significantly. When the Cross-Encoder evaluated the pair, it promoted Section 2.4 to \textbf{Rank \#1} (+0.4351), placing the exception at the very front of the prompt:
\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Answer 2 (Re-Ranked):} \textit{``Yes, staff can fly in premium cabins for international client negotiations under the long-haul international travel exception clause. The policy allows this exception when traveling on continuous non-stop international flights with flight durations exceeding 8 hours or 6 hours if the traveler is scheduled to deliver a client presentation or attend executive contract negotiations within 12 hours of flight arrival. Employees need prior written concurrence from both the Division Vice President and the Chief Financial Officer no later than 10 business days before booking their tickets.''}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Detailed Case Study 2: Negation \& Distractor Chunk (TEST\_02)}
\textbf{Query:} \textit{``What items are not covered on expense claims for client business meals?''}\\
\textbf{Target Rule:} Section 5.3 restricts alcohol to 1 drink per diner, prohibits solo dining alcohol, and explicitly bans nightclub bottle service, adult entertainment, tobacco, and gambling losses.

In the baseline retrieval, the dense retriever matched ``client business meals'' to Section 5.1 at Rank \#1 (0.5936). For the negative constraint ``not covered'', it pulled Section 3.2 (\textit{Prohibited Hotel Incidentals}) at Rank \#2 (0.5562). The actual meal prohibition clause (Section 5.3) sat at Rank \#3 (0.4615).
\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Answer 1 (Baseline Distraction Error):} \textit{``Based on the policy context provided, the following items are not covered on expense claims for client business meals: In-room video on-demand/movie rentals, Mini-bar consumption, Laundry service for trips shorter than 5 days, Spa services, Sauna access, Dry cleaning fees, Hotel gym or fitness center pass fees...''}
\end{quote}
This was not an ungrounded hallucination; the 0.5B model faithfully copied items from a chunk it was given, but it was misled by a higher-ranked distractor (Section 3.2). The Cross-Encoder resolved this by recognizing that Section 5.3 directly matches meal and entertainment exclusions, promoting Section 5.3 to \textbf{Rank \#2} (+1.1171) ahead of Section 3.2:
\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Answer 2 (Re-Ranked Corrected):} \textit{``Based on the policy context provided, the items that are not covered on expense claims for client business meals are: (a) Alcoholic beverages exceeding one (1) standard single serving of wine or beer per person during authorized client dinners. (b) Adult entertainment venues, nightclub admission or table bottle service fees, social club charges, and gentlemen's club invoices. (c) Tobacco, vape products, cannabis, and casino gambling losses. (d) Meals or travel for spouses, partners, dependents, or personal companions accompanying the traveler.''}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Detailed Case Studies 3 \& 4: Buried Clause Omission Failures (TEST\_03 \& TEST\_04)}
Tests 3 and 4 represent genuine retrieval omission failures where the target clause was buried outside the baseline Top 3 context window and rescued into the Top 3 by the Cross-Encoder.

In \textbf{TEST\_03} (\textit{``Under what trip length can travelers expense laundry and dry cleaning?''}), Section 3.2 specifies that laundry is non-reimbursable for trips shorter than 5 days. Dense search buried Section 3.2 at \textbf{Rank \#5} (0.3667), loading only filing deadlines (Section 8.1) and pre-trip authorization (Section 1.2) into the baseline prompt. Lacking the clause, the baseline LLM fabricated an answer: \textit{``...travelers cannot expense laundry and dry cleaning for trips longer than 30 days.''} The Cross-Encoder promoted Section 3.2 directly to \textbf{Rank \#1} (+2.4859), and the re-ranked model answered correctly: \textit{``For trips shorter than 5 days, travelers cannot expense laundry and dry cleaning.''}

In \textbf{TEST\_04} (\textit{``Are flight cancellation insurance and seat selection fees reimbursable?''}), Section 2.3 governs baggage allowances and ancillary airline fees. Dense retrieval buried Section 2.3 at \textbf{Rank \#8} (0.3413) due to vocabulary mismatch. The Cross-Encoder promoted Section 2.3 to \textbf{Rank \#2} (-1.6741), rescuing the chunk into the prompt context.

\subsection{Control Cases (TEST\_05 \& TEST\_06)}
In TEST\_05 (airport rideshare vs limousine ban in Section 4.4) and TEST\_06 (30-day late expense submission penalty in Section 8.5), the semantic overlap was already strong. Section 4.4 remained at Rank \#2 in both pipelines, and Section 8.5 stayed at Rank \#1 in both pipelines. These control cases verify that the Cross-Encoder does not destabilize rankings when the dense retriever already finds the correct chunk.

\subsection{Latency Trade-Off Analysis}
The dense baseline runs in $\approx 10.2$\,ms on CPU, with roughly 9.7\,ms spent on query embedding generation and under 0.5\,ms on cosine dot products across the 30 chunks. Re-ranking 10 candidate pairs through the Cross-Encoder added an average of 141.6\,ms ($\approx 14.2$\,ms per candidate pair), bringing total retrieval time to $\approx 151.8$\,ms.

While a $14.9\times$ overhead sounds large, an end-to-end retrieval latency of 152\,ms remains well within the standard 200\,ms threshold for interactive user search. On a dedicated GPU server, batched Cross-Encoder inference is projected to drop to an estimated 15--25\,ms total ($\approx 2.0$\,ms per candidate pair), bringing total retrieval runtime under 35\,ms.

\section{Scaling: Why Not Run Cross-Encoder Over the Whole Collection?}

If Cross-Encoders are noticeably more accurate, why not bypass the Bi-Encoder entirely and score every chunk in the collection with a Cross-Encoder?

\textbf{Compute Cost and Quadratic Complexity:} A Cross-Encoder must perform full bidirectional self-attention across the concatenated sequence length $L = |q| + |c|$, which has quadratic complexity $\mathcal{O}(L^2)$. Scoring a collection of $N$ passages requires running $N$ complete transformer forward passes. Based on my measured CPU runtime of 14.2\,ms per pair, evaluating a single query over a modest corporate corpus of $N = 1,000,000$ chunks would take $1,000,000 \times 14.2\,\text{ms} \approx 3.9\,\text{hours}$. Even on a modern GPU with an estimated latency of 2.0\,ms per pair, a full linear scan would take over 33 minutes per query. Under interactive user traffic, this is completely infeasible.

\textbf{Impossibility of Offline Indexing:} With a Bi-Encoder, passage embeddings are calculated once offline and indexed into sub-linear Approximate Nearest Neighbor graph structures (e.g., HNSW in FAISS), allowing millisecond search across millions of documents. In contrast, a Cross-Encoder requires both the query and passage simultaneously to compute attention. Because the query is unknown until runtime, no precomputed index can be constructed, forcing an exhaustive $\mathcal{O}(N)$ scan over every single passage.

\textbf{The Two-Stage Solution:} The two-stage funnel reconciles this trade-off by using the Bi-Encoder to prune 99.9\% of the corpus in milliseconds, after which the Cross-Encoder evaluates only the top 10 candidates with token-level cross-attention.
